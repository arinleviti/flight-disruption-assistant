# Technical problems

Topic: whether a technical fault on the aircraft is an extraordinary circumstance.

## General rule: technical problems are NOT extraordinary
Covers: engine failure, hydraulic fault, a broken or failed part, aircraft repairs, maintenance, unscheduled inspections, aircraft grounded for repairs.

In Wallentin-Hermann v Alitalia (C-549/07, 22 December 2008), a flight was cancelled because of an engine problem discovered shortly before departure. The Court of Justice held that a technical problem in an aircraft that leads to a cancellation is not covered by the concept of extraordinary circumstances, unless the problem stems from events which, by their nature or origin, are not inherent in the normal exercise of the airline's activity and are beyond its actual control.

The reasoning: airlines are confronted with technical problems as a matter of course. Maintaining aircraft and fixing faults, including unexpected ones discovered during inspections, is part of normal airline operations. The fact that the airline complied with the minimum maintenance rules does not by itself prove extraordinary circumstances.

Typical examples that are NOT extraordinary: faults found during pre-flight inspections, hydraulic, engine or system failures, unscheduled maintenance, a spare part that is not available.

## Narrow exceptions
Covers: hidden manufacturing defect across a fleet, sabotage, terrorism, an object lying on the runway, foreign object damage.

A technical problem can be extraordinary only if it comes from something outside normal operations, for example:
- a hidden manufacturing defect revealed by the manufacturer or a competent authority that affects a whole fleet;
- damage caused by sabotage or terrorism;
- damage caused by an external object the airline could not control, such as foreign object damage: in Germanwings v Pauels (C-501/17, 4 April 2019), damage to a tyre caused by a screw lying on the runway was held to be an extraordinary circumstance.

Even in these cases the airline must show it took all reasonable measures to limit the consequences (for example, how quickly it repaired the aircraft or found a replacement).

## How to apply it
Ask where the fault came from. If it arose inside the aircraft or from its maintenance, it is part of normal operations: NOT extraordinary. Only an external cause, beyond the airline's control, can make it extraordinary.

Sources: CJEU, Wallentin-Hermann v Alitalia, C-549/07, 22 December 2008; CJEU, Germanwings v Pauels, C-501/17, 4 April 2019.
