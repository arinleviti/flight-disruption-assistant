# Strikes

Topic: whether a strike is an extraordinary circumstance. The answer depends on WHOSE strike it is.

## Strikes by the airline's own staff: NOT extraordinary
Covers: industrial action by the airline's own cabin crew, pilots or employees; the airline's own staff going on strike, walking out, calling in sick en masse or refusing to fly; not enough crew available because of a strike; a union strike against the airline over pay or working conditions.

**Wildcat strike (Krüsemann and Others v TUIfly, joined cases C-195/17 and others, 17 April 2018).** After the airline's management made a surprise announcement of a restructuring, a large part of its flight crew reported sick for about a week, causing many cancellations and long delays. The Court of Justice held that this "wildcat strike" was not an extraordinary circumstance: the risks arising from the social consequences of such management decisions are part of the normal exercise of an airline's activity, the protest was triggered by the airline's own decision, and it ended when the airline reached an agreement with the staff representatives. It was therefore not beyond the airline's actual control.

**Strike called by a trade union (Airhelp v SAS, C-28/20, Grand Chamber, 23 March 2021).** SAS pilots in Denmark, Sweden and Norway went on a lawful strike, called by their unions after negotiations on a collective agreement failed. The Court held that a strike by the airline's own staff, aimed at obtaining things only the airline can give (pay, working conditions), is not an extraordinary circumstance. Such negotiations are part of normal business, and an airline facing a strike announced in advance keeps control over it: it can prepare and limit the consequences.

This covers strikes by the airline's pilots, cabin crew, or other employees, whether lawful or spontaneous.

## External strikes: CAN be extraordinary
Covers: air traffic controllers (ATC) on strike or refusing to work, airport staff, security staff or baggage handlers on strike, any strike by people who do not work for the airline.

The strikes mentioned in recital 14 of the regulation as possible extraordinary circumstances are strikes external to the airline's activity, such as strikes by air traffic controllers or airport staff. These are beyond the airline's control: it is not a party to the dispute and cannot end it.

The Court also indicated in Airhelp v SAS that a strike by the airline's own staff could be extraordinary if it originated from demands that only public authorities can satisfy, because then it would be beyond the airline's control.

Even with an external strike, the airline must show it took all reasonable measures, for example re-routing passengers at the earliest opportunity.

## How to apply it
- Strike by the airline's own crew or employees, against the airline: NOT extraordinary.
- Strike by air traffic controllers, airport staff or other third parties: generally extraordinary.

Sources: CJEU, Krüsemann and Others v TUIfly, C-195/17 and others, 17 April 2018; CJEU, Airhelp v Scandinavian Airlines System, C-28/20, 23 March 2021; Regulation (EC) No 261/2004, recital 14.
